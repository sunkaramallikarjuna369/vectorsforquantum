What Are Vectors?

Vectors are fundamental mathematical objects that serve as the backbone of both classical physics and quantum mechanics.

Classical Context

In classical physics, vectors represent quantities with both magnitude and direction:

  • Force: Push or pull with specific direction
  • Velocity: Speed with direction of motion
  • Displacement: Distance with direction from origin
  • Momentum: Mass times velocity vector
Example: A force of 10 Newtons pointing northeast can be represented as a vector with magnitude 10 and direction 45°.

Quantum Context

In quantum computing, vectors take on a more abstract but equally powerful role:

  • Quantum States: Represented as vectors in complex Hilbert space
  • Superposition: Linear combinations of basis vectors
  • Probability Amplitudes: Vector components encode measurement probabilities
  • Entanglement: Multi-dimensional vector spaces for coupled systems

Vectors in Quantum Computing

Key Insight: In quantum computing, a quantum state is represented by a column vector whose elements are complex probability amplitudes.

Hilbert Space

Quantum states exist in an abstract complex vector space called Hilbert space. This is a complete inner product space where:

  • Vectors represent quantum states
  • Vector addition represents superposition
  • Inner products give probability amplitudes
  • Normalization ensures total probability = 1

Quantum State Representation

A quantum state |ψ⟩ is represented as a column vector:

|ψ⟩ = [α₀, α₁, α₂, ..., αₙ]ᵀ

Where:

  • αᵢ are complex probability amplitudes
  • |αᵢ|² gives the probability of measuring state i
  • Σ|αᵢ|² = 1 (normalization condition)

Single Qubit Example

A qubit (quantum bit) is represented by a 2-dimensional vector:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ = [α, β]ᵀ

Where |α|² + |β|² = 1

Example: The state |ψ⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩ represents a qubit in equal superposition with 50% probability of measuring 0 and 50% probability of measuring 1.

Probability Amplitudes and Measurement

The connection between vectors and quantum measurement is fundamental:

Concept Mathematical Representation Physical Meaning
Quantum State |ψ⟩ = Σᵢ αᵢ|i⟩ Superposition of basis states
Amplitude αᵢ ∈ ℂ Complex coefficient for state |i⟩
Probability P(i) = |αᵢ|² Probability of measuring state i
Normalization Σᵢ |αᵢ|² = 1 Total probability must equal 1

The Measurement Process

When we measure a quantum state:

  1. Before measurement: The state exists in superposition (all amplitudes contribute)
  2. During measurement: The state "collapses" to one of the basis states
  3. After measurement: We observe a definite classical outcome with probability |αᵢ|²
Born Rule: The probability of measuring a particular state is given by the square of the magnitude of its amplitude. This is the fundamental link between the quantum (vector) description and classical (probabilistic) observations.

Classical vs Quantum Vectors

Classical Vectors

  • Components are real numbers
  • Represent physical quantities (force, velocity)
  • Magnitude has direct physical meaning
  • Direction is in physical space
Classical Example:
Velocity vector: v⃗ = [3, 4] m/s
Magnitude: |v⃗| = 5 m/s
Direction: 53.13° from x-axis

Quantum Vectors

  • Components are complex numbers
  • Represent quantum states in Hilbert space
  • Magnitude squared gives probability
  • "Direction" is in abstract state space
Quantum Example:
Qubit state: |ψ⟩ = [0.6, 0.8i]
P(0) = |0.6|² = 0.36 (36%)
P(1) = |0.8i|² = 0.64 (64%)

Why Vectors Matter in Quantum Computing

Vectors are not just a convenient notation—they are essential to quantum computing:

1. State Representation
Every quantum state can be uniquely represented as a vector in Hilbert space. This provides a complete mathematical description of the quantum system.
2. Superposition
Vector addition naturally represents quantum superposition. A quantum state can be in multiple classical states simultaneously, represented as a linear combination of basis vectors.
3. Quantum Operations
Quantum gates are linear transformations (matrices) acting on state vectors. This makes quantum algorithms mathematically tractable.
4. Measurement Theory
Inner products between vectors give probability amplitudes for measurement outcomes. This connects the quantum formalism to observable results.
5. Entanglement
Multi-qubit systems are represented by tensor products of vectors, naturally describing quantum correlations that have no classical analogue.

Python Example: Basic Vector Representation

import numpy as np

# Classical vector (real components)
classical_vector = np.array([3, 4])
magnitude = np.linalg.norm(classical_vector)
print(f"Classical vector: {classical_vector}")
print(f"Magnitude: {magnitude}")

# Quantum state vector (complex components)
# Representing |ψ⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩
quantum_state = np.array([1/np.sqrt(2), 1/np.sqrt(2)], dtype=complex)
print(f"\nQuantum state: {quantum_state}")

# Calculate probabilities
probabilities = np.abs(quantum_state)**2
print(f"P(0) = {probabilities[0]:.3f}")
print(f"P(1) = {probabilities[1]:.3f}")
print(f"Total probability = {np.sum(probabilities):.3f}")

# Another quantum state with complex amplitudes
psi = np.array([0.6, 0.8j], dtype=complex)
print(f"\nComplex quantum state: {psi}")
print(f"P(0) = {np.abs(psi[0])**2:.3f}")
print(f"P(1) = {np.abs(psi[1])**2:.3f}")
print(f"Normalized: {np.isclose(np.sum(np.abs(psi)**2), 1)}")

Output:

Classical vector: [3 4]
Magnitude: 5.0

Quantum state: [0.70710678+0.j 0.70710678+0.j]
P(0) = 0.500
P(1) = 0.500
Total probability = 1.000

Complex quantum state: [0.6+0.j  0. +0.8j]
P(0) = 0.360
P(1) = 0.640
Normalized: True

Key Takeaways

  • Vectors are the mathematical foundation of quantum computing
  • Quantum states are represented as vectors in complex Hilbert space
  • Vector components are complex probability amplitudes
  • The square of amplitude magnitude gives measurement probability
  • Normalization ensures total probability equals 1
  • Vector operations (addition, inner product) have direct quantum interpretations
  • Understanding vectors is essential for understanding quantum algorithms
